Phase 2: Systematic Grammar Rules

The Core Rules of Comparison

Compare things or qualities with adjectives, and actions with adverbs.

A

Comparative Adjectives

We use comparative adjectives to compare two things, people, or places. They can come before nouns or after linking verbs (such as is, seem, become, prove) and verbs of perception (find).

1. One-Syllable Adjectives: add -er
cold → colder old → older slow → slower fast → faster
2. Long Adjectives (3+ syllables): use more / less
interesting → more interesting successful → more successful
3. Adjectives after "find" or "make"

"Fans found the game more interesting than the last one."
"The winner was 14 years older than the runner-up."

Remember: Do not combine both (*more colder is incorrect; write colder).
B

Comparative Adverbs

We use comparative adverbs to describe how an action is done (modifying action verbs like swim, cycle, run, jump, train).

1. Adverbs ending in -ly: use more / less
gracefully → more gracefully quickly → more quickly

"He accepted defeat more gracefully than his team."

2. Frequency Adverb: often

The comparative form of often is more often (not *oftener):
"She practised more often than her teammates."

3. Short Adverbs (same as adjectives)

Some words keep the short -er form as adverbs:
slow → slower ("He swam much slower than he cycled.")
fast → faster, hard → harder.

Action check: To describe how someone swims, jumps, or runs, use an adverb form.
IMPORTANT FOCUS

Two-Syllable Adjectives: Ending in -y vs Other Two-Syllable Adjectives

Students often wonder whether a two-syllable adjective takes -er or more. Here is the simple distinction:

1. Two Syllables Ending in -y → -ier

When a two-syllable adjective ends in the letter -y, change the -y to -i and add -er:

steady → steadier ("a steadier pace")
heavy → heavier ("heavier rainfall")
early → earlier ("an earlier start time")
easy → easier ("an easier test")
2. Most Other Two-Syllable Words → more + adjective

Two-syllable adjectives that do not end in -y usually take more:

modern → more modern ("a more modern facility")
recent → more recent ("more recent figures")
crowded → more crowded ("a more crowded stadium")
useful → more useful ("more useful data")
KEY IRREGULARS

Irregular Comparative Forms You Must Know

well → better

Adverb of action quality:
"He ran well: much better than last year."

far → farther / further

Distance or degree:
"The skiers had to jump much farther than before."

bad / badly → worse

Negative result or performance:
"The team performed worse in wet weather."

Essential Concept

What are 'Modifiers'?

Explaining how big or small the difference between two things is.

[Modifier] + [Comparative Word]

"In this sentence from the sample answer the word significantly is used to modify the comparative. In other words, to say how big or small the comparison is. Here, the word significantly is called a modifier."

1. Big Differences Large Gap

When one number or result is much higher or lower than another, use:

much significantly substantially far considerably
• "He swam much slower than he cycled."
• "Cold countries were significantly more successful."
• "The champion leapt far farther than her rivals."
2. Small / Close Differences Narrow Gap

When two results are very close to each other, use:

slightly a little a bit marginally
• "She finished slightly faster than the second runner."
• "His race time was a little better than yesterday."
• "The score was only marginally higher."
