The Undergraduate Shift

Back in secondary school, life followed a predictable rhythm. My teachers would remind us about upcoming deadlines every single afternoon, so I never needed a planner. I used to believe that higher education would just be an extended version of those school years. However, when I enrolled in chemical engineering last autumn, that comfortable illusion disappeared overnight.

During my first month, I found the autonomy overwhelming. I was definitely not used to managing four-hour lab sessions alongside self-directed literature reviews. At first, I couldn't get used to the noisy atmosphere of the library's collaborative floor, so I retreated to the silent basement archives. Whenever exam week approached in high school, our family would gather for quiet dinners, but in halls of residence, flatmates were constantly cooking at midnight.

Now that my second term is underway, things feel natural. I have finally got used to organizing my weekly timetable independently, and I am completely used to the intense pace of seminar debates. I still smile when I recall how I used to panic over a single ten-page reading assignment!

Syntactic & Semantic Contrasts

These four constructions express distinct concepts regarding past time, past states, adaptation, and familiar routines.

Structure Meaning / Function Syntactic Rule Academic Example
used to + infinitive Past habits OR past states that are no longer true today. Subject + used to + Base Verb
(Negative: didn't use to)		 "The faculty used to have its own dedicated archive before digitalization."
would + infinitive Repeated, characteristic past actions (habits/routines).
Cannot be used with state verbs.		 Subject + would + Base Verb "Every Friday afternoon, our tutor would review our research drafts."
be used to + -ing / noun Being accustomed to something; feeling that a condition is normal and familiar. Subject + be + used to + [Noun / Gerund] "Postgraduate scholars are used to working late into the evening."
get used to + -ing / noun The transition or process of becoming accustomed to something new. Subject + [tenses of get] + used to + [Noun / Gerund] "International candidates usually get used to the climate within a month."

Key Usage Nuances to Master

1. Stative vs. Dynamic Verbs with 'Would':
We say: "We used to live on campus" (State = live), NEVER "We would live on campus." Reserve would strictly for recurring actions (e.g., "We would walk to campus together every morning.").
2. The Dual Nature of "to":
In used to + infinitive, "to" is part of the infinitive marker (followed by base verb). In be/get used to, "to" is a preposition, which is why it requires a noun phrase or a gerund (-ing form).
3. Time Horizon:
Used to and would are strictly anchored in the past. Be used to and get used to can be conjugated across all tenses (Past, Present, Present Perfect, Future).
