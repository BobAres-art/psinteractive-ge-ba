The Grand Opening of Atelier Lumière

Read the narrative naturally. Pay close attention to how actions were arranged and who actually performed them.

When Elena decided to open Atelier Lumière, an upscale creative venue downtown, she knew she couldn’t handle every task alone. Three weeks before the opening gala, Elena had the painters coat the interior walls in warm champagne tones. Because the vintage fixtures were delicate, she got a certified electrician to restore the antique brass chandeliers so that they would shine safely.

Time was running short, so Elena also had the hardwood floor polished by a specialized restoration crew. The original catering company cancelled abruptly, but after several persistent phone calls, Elena managed to get a renowned pastry chef to prepare sixty custom dessert towers.

On the final morning, she had her assistant print the embossed guest lists and got the audio system calibrated by a professional sound engineer. When the doors finally opened and the jazz quartet played, guests praised the spotless venue. Elena smiled gracefully: she had not picked up a single wrench or paintbrush herself, yet through coordination and persuasion, she had brought the entire vision to life.

Active 'Get': Subject + get + person + to-verb

Essential Framework

The Architecture of Causatives

We use causative verbs when we cause someone else to take an action, or when we arrange for a professional service rather than doing it ourselves. The two pillars are Active Causation (focus on the agent) and Passive Causation (focus on the task accomplished).

Type A: Active Focus on the Doer

Active Causatives: Have & Get

You identify the person performing the action. Notice the crucial grammatical difference between the two verbs:

1. HAVE + Person + Base Verb (Bare Infinitive)

Delegating / Authority

Giving someone the job, responsibility, or customary instruction.

Formula: Subject + HAVE + Person + Base Form (do)

"I had the mechanic inspect the engine before our trip."

2. GET + Person + TO-Infinitive

Persuading / Convincing

Overcoming resistance, encouraging, or convincing someone to act.

Formula: Subject + GET + Person + TO + Verb

"She finally got her brother to lend her his camera."

Memory Trigger: G-E-T pairs with T-O (Get ... TO do). HAVE prefers no 'to'.
Type B: Passive Focus on the Outcome

Passive Causative: Have / Get Something Done

Used when the exact person doing the job is obvious, unimportant, or when emphasizing the finished product:

Formula: Subject + HAVE/GET + Object + Past Participle (V3)

Both have and get can be used interchangeably here, though get is slightly more informal and emphasizes effort.

Subject + HAVE / GET + Object (Thing) + Past Participle (V3)
  • "We had the roof repaired after the storm."
  • "I must get my passport renewed next week."

Bonus Meaning: Unexpected Misfortunes

The passive structure is also used when something unpleasant happens to someone's property (the subject did not cause it):

"Marcus had his wallet stolen on the crowded subway."

Golden Rule: Whenever the item directly follows have/get (e.g. hair, phone, roof, car), use the Past Participle!
